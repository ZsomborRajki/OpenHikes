Privacy Policy

This privacy policy applies to the OpenHikes app for mobile devices, together with any related services operated by Zsombor Rajki (collectively, the "Application"). Zsombor Rajki is hereby referred to as the "Service Provider".

Summary

OpenHikes has no account and no login. What you record stays on your device and, if you leave iCloud sync enabled, syncs only to your own private iCloud storage, which the Service Provider cannot read. The Application contains no analytics, no advertising, and no tracking of any kind.

There is one exception, and it happens only when you ask for it: sharing a hike with the community, or adding your photographs to a trail somebody has already shared. Choosing to share publishes that hike — its route, its photographs, and a name you type — to a shared database the Service Provider operates, so that other people using the Application can find it. Nothing is shared unless you tap Share on a specific hike and confirm. Everything else in this policy describes an Application that sends the Service Provider nothing, and that remains true if you never share a hike. See Sharing a hike with the community and Adding your photographs to a trail that is already there.

What information does the Application obtain and how is it used?

The Application does not require registration. Apart from a hike you explicitly choose to share, it collects no personal information on behalf of the Service Provider. It does create and store data on your device in order to work:

  • Hikes you record or import, including route coordinates, distance, elevation, timing, the title you give a hike, any notes you write about it, and any description, author, or keywords included in an imported GPX file.
  • Photographs you take or attach within the Application, together with when and where they were taken.
  • Your settings and preferences, and any offline map tiles you have chosen to download.
  • Local diagnostic and performance measurements, used to investigate battery use and responsiveness. These are written to the Application’s own storage on your device and are never transmitted anywhere.

This data lives in the Application’s storage on your device. It is not sent to the Service Provider, who has no means of accessing it.

Does the Application collect precise real time location information of the device?

Yes. Recording a hike requires precise, real-time location — that is what a recorded trail is. The Application uses location in four distinct ways:

  • Showing where you are on the map. The Application asks for “While Using the App” location access the first time you open it, not the first time you record. This is what puts your position on the map and lets features centre on you. You can decline it and still browse maps and past hikes; recording a hike is what actually requires it.
  • Recording a hike. While a recording is running, the Application receives precise location continuously and keeps receiving it when you leave the Application or lock the phone, so that the trail does not stop at your pocket. iOS shows its own background-location indicator while this is happening. This does not depend on the Background Trail Tracking switch below. The points are written into the hike on your device.
  • Background Trail Tracking (optional, off by default). Turning this on in Settings is what asks for “Always” location access. It uses occasional, low-power updates to keep your Home Screen widget showing your progress along a selected trail while the Application is not open. It asks iOS for those updates only while you have a trail selected and iOS reports your phone to be within about ten kilometres of it, or while a walk along one is under way, so a trail you left selected months ago in another country costs you nothing. To make that decision the Application registers the area around the selected trail with iOS and is told when your phone enters or leaves it; it does not receive, and does not store, your position for this purpose. It is a separate feature from recording, and turning it off does not affect recording.
  • Reminders while a hike is paused (“Remind Me to Pause and Resume”, on by default). When a recording is paused, the Application keeps watching your location so that it can notice you have set off again — about half a kilometre from where you paused, or a quarter of one at cycling pace — and send you a notification offering to resume. If you have granted “Always” access it does this with the coarse significant-change updates iOS already produces, at no additional battery cost and with no location indicator shown. With only “While Using the App” access it has to keep a coarse location feed running for the length of the pause, and iOS keeps showing the background-location indicator, which is accurate: location really is still in use. The same reminder covers a paused trail walk, using location the Application is already receiving. Locations used for this are read only to make that decision. They are not added to the recorded hike and not drawn on the map, and turning the switch off in Settings stops the watching entirely.

Location data is written to your device. Except for the route of a hike you choose to share, it is not transmitted to the Service Provider, is not sold, is not shared for advertising, and is not used for any purpose beyond the four above. You can review, downgrade, or revoke location access at any time in the iOS Settings app, and the Application’s own switches for Background Trail Tracking and pause reminders are in its Settings screen. Hikes already recorded remain on your device until you delete them.

Does the Application use iCloud?

If iCloud sync is left enabled, your hikes are mirrored to your own private iCloud database so that they appear on your other devices. This uses Apple’s CloudKit service and your personal Apple Account. The data is held in your private storage, not in any database belonging to the Service Provider, and the Service Provider cannot read, retrieve, or delete it. Apple’s handling of that data is governed by Apple’s own privacy policy. You can turn iCloud sync off in the Application’s settings.

Photographs

Photographs you take within the Application are stored with the hike on your device. If you turn on “Also Save to Photos”, a copy is additionally written to your photo library. The “Find Photos of This Hike” feature reads your photo library on the device, limited to pictures taken between the first and last point of the hike you are viewing, in order to pin them to the trail; it does not browse your wider library and it does not upload anything. Camera and photo library access can be revoked at any time in the iOS Settings app.

Health

If you turn on “Save Hikes to Health”, a hike you finish recording is also written to the Health app on your device as a hiking workout, carrying its distance, its duration, its elevation gained and its route. This is off until you turn it on, and turning it on is what asks for permission. The Application only ever writes to Health — it never reads anything from it, and it never asks for read access. What is written stays in your own Health store on your own device; the Service Provider cannot see it, and nothing about it is uploaded anywhere. You can revoke the Application’s Health access at any time in the iOS Settings app or in the Health app, and workouts already written stay in Health until you delete them there.

Sharing a hike with the community

The Application lets you publish one of your hikes so that other people using it can find and save that trail. This is entirely optional, is never automatic, and applies only to hikes you explicitly choose to share.

What is sent when you share a hike:

  • The route as it was recorded: its coordinates, elevations, distance, the date of the walk, and the time each point along it was reached — which is also a record of how long the walk took and how fast it was walked.
  • The hike’s title, and its notes if it has any — whatever you wrote on the sharing screen, or the description an imported GPX file came with. The sharing screen shows you both before you send, because a file you imported may carry words you have never read.
  • Up to thirty-six of the photographs attached to that hike, along with the point on the trail and the time each was taken at. Photographs are resized before they are sent, and their embedded camera metadata — including the camera’s own GPS position, which is more precise than anything the Application shows — is removed. The time is sent alongside the picture rather than inside it, so that the photographs stay in order for whoever saves the hike. The originals on your device are not changed.
  • The name you type in the sharing screen. You choose this name; it is not taken from your Apple Account. You may leave it blank and share without a name.

What this means: a shared hike goes to a public database operated by the Service Provider using Apple’s CloudKit. Unlike the private iCloud sync described above, the Service Provider can read this data, and so — once published — can anybody else using the Application. Apple records which Apple Account submitted each shared hike, and the Service Provider can see that identifier. This is what makes it possible to review submissions and to remove one on request.

Review before publication: a hike you send is not published, listed, or searchable by anyone until a person has reviewed it, and nothing in the Application can find it in the meantime. Every submission is reviewed by a person before it is published, in order to keep inaccurate or inappropriate content out of the Application. When you send a hike the Application says only that it has been sent, because at that moment that is all it knows. Afterwards it can find out one thing on its own: whether a published listing exists for your submission. It asks that when you open that hike, and remembers a yes — so a hike you shared reads as waiting for review until it appears publicly and as published to the community once it does. What it cannot tell you is the difference between a submission nobody has looked at yet and one a reviewer decided not to publish: a decision not to publish leaves nothing behind for the Application to see, so both keep reading as waiting. A submission cannot be altered once it is sent — not by you and not by anyone using a modified copy of the Application — so what is reviewed is what other people later download. A reviewer may make two corrections before publishing it, and no others: they may publish the hike under a corrected title, which does not change the title you sent; and they may leave out individual photographs, which deletes those photographs and publishes the rest of the hike as you sent it. Both happen before anything is published, and the alternative to either was declining the whole submission. Your route, your description and the name you typed are never edited. A shared hike cannot be changed, and the Application will not send a second copy of one. To replace a hike you have shared, ask for it to be removed — see Removal below — and share the corrected walk once it has been taken down.

Please do not share what you do not want published. A shared route reveals where you walked, and a trail that begins at your home reveals where you live. Photographs of identifiable people should not be shared without their agreement.

Removal: to have a hike you shared taken down, open it in the Application, tap the community button on its screen and choose Ask for Removal. The Application composes the message — the hike’s title, the date of the walk, whether it has been published, and the record names that identify it in the database — and opens it in your mail app so that you can read and send it. It carries nothing about you beyond whatever your mail app puts on a message you send. You can also email zsombor.rajki@gmail.com directly, with enough detail to identify the hike. Removing the hike from your own device does not withdraw a submission you have already sent — which is why deleting a shared hike offers Ask for Removal First before it goes. The Application is not told when a hike is taken down, so a hike you have asked about goes on reading as published to the community until you check: the same menu offers Check Whether It’s Still Live, which asks the database once and, if the hike is gone, offers to reset it on your device so that a corrected walk can be shared in its place. Resetting forgets the record names a removal request quotes, so it is offered only once the hike is already gone.

Reporting somebody else’s hike: every published hike has a menu on the screen that shows it, offering Report. Tapping it asks what is wrong — offensive or inappropriate content, a private place or an identifiable person, something published without permission, or a dangerous or misleading route — and lets you add anything that helps. The report is prepared as an email to zsombor.rajki@gmail.com and opens in your mail app so that you can read and send it; it identifies the hike being reported, and it carries nothing about you beyond whatever your mail app puts on a message you send. You do not need an Apple Account to report a hike, just as you do not need one to browse. Reports are read and acted on within 24 hours, and a hike found to be objectionable is removed for everybody.

Blocking somebody: the same menu offers Block. Blocking hides every hike that person has shared, and every hike they share later, from the Application on that device. It is kept on the device and nowhere else: it is not sent to the Service Provider, it does not travel to your other devices, and it removes nothing for anybody else. It needs no Apple Account. The people you have blocked are listed under Blocked in Settings, where each entry can be undone. Blocking is not a report — to have a hike taken down for everybody, report it.

Saving somebody else’s hike: when you save a shared hike to your own list, its route and photographs are downloaded to your device. Downloaded photographs are never added to your photo library.

Adding your photographs to a trail that is already there

A trail you find in the Application may already be listed — because somebody published it, or because it is one of the signposted routes OpenStreetMap knows about. If you save that trail, walk it and photograph it, the Application offers to add your photographs to the trail that is already there rather than publishing a second copy of the same route. This is optional and never automatic: it happens when you tap the community button on your own copy of the hike and send them.

What is sent when you add photographs:

  • Up to thirty-six of the photographs attached to your hike, along with the point on the trail and the time each was taken at. They are resized before they are sent and their embedded camera metadata — including the camera’s own GPS position — is removed, exactly as for a shared hike. The originals on your device are not changed.
  • Which trail they are for, and the name you type as a credit. You may leave the name blank.

Your walk itself is not sent. Your route, your notes and what you called the hike stay on your device. That is the whole difference between this and sharing a hike: the trail is already public, so what is published is the photographs and nothing else.

The same review, and the same removal. Photographs you add are not visible to anybody until a person has reviewed them, and the Application says only that they have been sent until it can see that a published record exists for them. A reviewer may leave out individual photographs before publishing the rest, which deletes the ones they leave out; they can change nothing else, because there is nothing else. To have photographs you added taken down, open your own copy of the hike, tap the community button and choose Ask for Removal — it names the photographs and not the trail, which belongs to somebody else.

Reporting and blocking work on the photograph. Photographs somebody added are shown alongside the trail’s own, each with the name of whoever added it. Opening one offers Report Photo and Block, and both are about that person rather than about whoever published the trail: reporting asks for those photographs to be removed and leaves the trail alone, and blocking hides that person’s photographs everywhere in the Application on that device. The same 24-hour commitment applies.

Please add only photographs you took, on that trail. A photograph taken at your home shows where you live, and photographs of identifiable people should not be published without their agreement.

Do third parties see and/or have access to information obtained by the Application?

Apart from a hike you choose to share, the Service Provider does not share your information, because the Service Provider does not receive it. However, some features require the Application to contact third-party services directly from your device, and those requests necessarily reveal something about the area you are looking at — and, when you search, the words you typed. Other than a shared hike, no account, name, or identifier is sent.

  • Map tiles — requests to tile.openstreetmap.org, tiles.stadiamaps.com, or tile.thunderforest.com, depending on the map style you have selected. Each request identifies the map square being displayed, which indicates approximately where you are looking. These are governed by the OpenStreetMap Foundation’s, Stadia Maps’, and Thunderforest’s own privacy policies.
  • Place search — the map’s search field is Apple’s. What you type is sent to Apple Maps as you type it, to offer suggestions, and the query you submit is sent again to look the place up and move the map to it. This happens whichever base map you have chosen: selecting OpenStreetMap, Stadia Maps or Thunderforest changes where the tiles come from and not where a search goes. No account or identifier of yours is attached; governed by Apple’s Apple Maps & Privacy policy. Apple Maps is also one of the base-map choices, in which case the map itself is drawn by Apple rather than from raster tiles.
  • Trail data — requests to overpass-api.de carry the bounding box of the area for which trail geometry is being looked up. This happens for two reasons: to work out what the trails under a hike you are looking at are made of, and to find the waymarked routes OpenStreetMap already knows about near an area you have asked to search. The second is described under Waymarked routes from OpenStreetMap below. Governed by the Overpass API operator’s own terms.
  • Weather — Apple WeatherKit is sent coordinates in order to return a forecast for a hike. The same request also asks for any severe-weather warnings issued for that place, and the Application may show you a notification when a severe one applies to the hike you are looking at; nothing further about you is sent in order to ask, and in parts of the world where Apple has no severe-weather source the Application says so rather than reporting that there are none. Governed by Apple’s privacy policy.
  • iCloud — as described above, both for your own private sync and, if you use the community feature, for shared hikes.
  • Shared hikes near the map — selecting the Community list asks Apple’s CloudKit for hikes near the part of the map you are looking at, which reveals that area to Apple in the same way a map tile request does. Nothing is asked before that. Afterwards, panning the map does not ask again on its own: it offers a Search this area button, and the area is sent only when you tap that. Leaving the Community list stops these requests. No Apple Account is needed.
  • Waymarked routes from OpenStreetMap — the same list also offers the signposted routes OpenStreetMap already knows about nearby. Those come from the Overpass API rather than from CloudKit, and asking for them sends the same area to a second service. This happens only when you tap Search this area (or Try Again after a failed search) — selecting the Community list, and the list refilling itself after you block somebody, ask Apple’s CloudKit alone. Not every hike in this list was published by somebody: the routes that come from OpenStreetMap have no author, no photographs and nothing of anybody’s to report or block, and each one links to its own page on openstreetmap.org. Routes you have looked at are kept on your device for thirty days so the same valley is not downloaded twice; they are deleted with the app and are never sent anywhere. Opening one of these routes asks Stadia Maps for the heights along it if you subscribe to OpenHikes Pro, because OpenStreetMap carries none: the coordinates of the public route are sent, up to two hundred of them, and nothing about you or where you are — the same route is asked about identically from anywhere in the world. It happens when you open the route, never for a list of them, and only while your subscription is active; the heights are drawn and thrown away with the screen unless you save the hike. Governed by Stadia Maps’ own privacy policy, the same one their map tiles are.
  • Shared hikes by name — what you type in the map’s search field is also sent to Apple’s CloudKit, shortly after you stop typing, to look for published hikes matching it. This is a separate request from Shared hikes near the map above and is not covered by it: it is made whether or not you have asked for community hikes near you, and leaving that list does not stop it. It reaches Apple’s CloudKit alone — OpenStreetMap has no index to search the world by name, so a typed search filters the routes the last Search this area already brought back and asks Overpass nothing. The text you type is what is sent; no Apple Account is needed.

These services may log request data, including your IP address, under their own policies. The Service Provider receives none of it.

What are my opt-out rights?

You can stop all collection by uninstalling the Application, except for a hike you have already chosen to share, which is withdrawn by email as described under Sharing a hike with the community. Sharing is off unless you use it: if you never tap Share on a hike, the Service Provider receives nothing at all. Within the Application you can delete individual hikes and their photographs, turn off iCloud sync, turn off Background Trail Tracking, and turn off “Remind Me to Pause and Resume”, which stops a paused hike being watched for movement. Location, camera, and photo library access can be revoked at any time in the iOS Settings app. Uninstalling removes the data the Application stored on your device; hikes already mirrored to your iCloud storage are removed through iCloud in the usual way.

If you contact the Service Provider directly or voluntarily provide information by other means, you may request deletion of that information by contacting zsombor.rajki@gmail.com.
